In 1954, subscribers to the Great Soviet Encyclopedia received a letter from the publisher. It came with replacement pages, and instructions to use a razor blade.

Volume two contained a three-page article on Lavrentiy Beria. Beria had been shot. The pages had to go.

What arrived to fill the gap was an expanded entry on the Bering Sea. History was corrected. The binding stayed exactly as thick as before.

That gets told as a story about the Soviet Union. It is really a story about chronicles, and it is very old.

Every chronicle was written by somebody. That somebody had a name, a patron, a city, and a firm opinion about who had started it.

The first surviving argument about how to write history is also the first argument between two historians.

Herodotus travelled, listened, and wrote down what people told him, including the parts he plainly did not believe himself.

Thucydides did the opposite. He narrowed his subject to a single war, distrusted hearsay, and wrote as though emotion were a contaminant.

He never names Herodotus. He does not need to. The disdain is legible from a distance of two and a half thousand years.

He was also candid about one limit. The speeches in his history are not transcripts. He wrote what each speaker would probably have said, keeping as near as he could to the general sense.

That is an unusually honest admission, and it means the founding document of rigorous history contains dialogue its author made up.

And it did not stop when they died. Roughly five centuries later, Plutarch wrote an entire essay attacking him.

The title, in the manuscripts, is On the Malice of Herodotus. Plutarch's central complaint is that Herodotus had been rude about Boeotia.

Plutarch was Boeotian.

In the sixth century, Procopius of Caesarea wrote the official account of Justinian's wars. It is admiring, detailed, and genuinely useful.

He also wrote a second book, which stayed unpublished while he lived. In that one, Justinian is a demon in human form, and the empress is worse.

Same man. Same events. Same decade. The difference is that only one of the two books could be read by the emperor.

He was not lying twice. He was writing for two audiences, and only one of them was in a position to have him killed.

Monastic chroniclers worked under a quieter pressure. Their houses held land, land was granted by kings, and kings occasionally read.

Bede, writing in Northumbria in the eighth century, produced a history of the English church, and in the process largely assembled the English.

There was no single English people sitting there waiting to be described. There were rival kingdoms, and a monk with a unifying idea.

Two centuries later the Peterborough Chronicle reaches the year 1137, lands in the middle of a civil war, and stops sounding administrative.

It describes torture, famine, and burned villages. It says that men spoke openly that Christ and his saints slept.

Then it adds that all this was suffered for our sins. The catastrophe was political. The explanation offered is theological. That swap is itself a position.

Around the same years, Geoffrey of Monmouth published a history of the kings of Britain, running the line back to the fall of Troy.

It handed Britain a royal descent older than Rome's, and handed the Norman court a past worth inheriting. A great deal of it he simply invented.

Some fifty years later, William of Newburgh opened his own history with a prologue given over almost entirely to demolishing Geoffrey.

Everything that man wrote, he said, was made up, either by himself or by others. He objected to the giants. He objected to the dragons.

He also objected to the dates, which suggests he had read the whole thing carefully before losing his temper.

It is the earliest surviving case of a historian beginning a book by attacking a dead colleague. It would not be the last.

In Kyiv, in roughly the same century, monks were compiling what we now call the Primary Chronicle. It opens by asking where the land of Rus came from.

Its answer is that the Slavic tribes, unable to keep order among themselves, sent across the sea and invited Varangian princes to come and rule them.

Whether anything like that happened is still argued. What is not in doubt is how useful such a sentence is to the dynasty already sitting on the throne.

Six hundred years later, that passage detonated.

In 1749 Gerhard Friedrich Müller, a German academician in Saint Petersburg, prepared an address deriving the very name Rus from a Scandinavian root.

Mikhail Lomonosov and others denounced it as derogatory to Russia. The address was already printed. The print run was destroyed.

A line written by a monk in the twelfth century had become, six centuries on, a question of state security.

For centuries the quarrel stayed indoors, inside the church and the court. Printing moved it into the market, where it got considerably louder.

In 1776 Edward Gibbon published the first volume of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, with a charge buried in chapters fifteen and sixteen.

Rome, he suggested, had not simply been overrun from outside. It had been hollowed out by a religion that taught its citizens to look elsewhere.

The clergy answered within the year. One reply appeared as An Apology for Christianity, in a series of letters, addressed to Edward Gibbon, Esquire.

The argument now had a postal address.

Then, in 1824, a young German named Leopold von Ranke wrote a preface that quietly rewrote the job description.

History, he said, had been assigned the office of judging the past. His own book did not aspire to that. It wanted only to show what actually happened.

Out of that came the apparatus we still use. Archives. Footnotes. The seminar. The demand that every claim carry a source you can go and check.

It was a real advance, and it is why modern history is checkable at all.

It was also built inside the Prussian state, staffed by its graduates, and paid by its ministries. Its instincts were reliably national.

The method was designed to remove the historian from the account. What it actually removed was the visible ones.

By the twentieth century that had become the whole quarrel. If the historian cannot be subtracted, what exactly is the thing being produced?

In 1931 Herbert Butterfield gave the problem a usable name. Whig history: reading the past as a run-up to the present, with the present as the point.

Every generation that finds its own arrangements foreshadowed in the twelfth century is doing it. Naming it did not stop anyone. It made it visible, which is a different service.

Two years earlier, in Strasbourg, Marc Bloch and Lucien Febvre had founded a journal to attack the same problem from underneath.

Kings and battles, they argued, were surface weather. Below them sat grain prices, roads, disease, climate, and what ordinary people actually believed.

Fernand Braudel later called that layer the longue durée: the history that moves so slowly nobody living inside it can feel it moving.

A failed Mediterranean harvest reshapes more lives than a coronation. It is simply harder to put in a chronicle, because it does not happen on any particular day.

Marc Bloch joined the French Resistance. He was captured, tortured, and shot by the Gestapo in 1944.

He left behind a short unfinished book asking what history is actually for, and what the historian's craft consists of.

In 1961 E. H. Carr compressed a century of suspicion into a single instruction. Study the historian, he wrote, before you begin to study the facts.

That sounds like cynicism. It is closer to a maintenance procedure. Nobody is telling you the account is worthless. You are being told to check who assembled it.

Which brings us to the genuinely strange thing about the present.

For almost all of this story, editing was invisible. A monk revised a chronicle, and the earlier version simply stopped existing.

A publisher mailed out replacement pages, and the old page went into the fire. The record changed, and the change left no record.

That is what has actually broken, and it is worth being precise about which part. Not the politics. The politics is a constant. The concealment.

An encyclopedia now keeps every version of itself it has ever had. Every edit is timestamped, attributed, and reversible.

The arguments happen on a page of their own, and that page is saved too. You can watch a dispute over a single sentence run for years and read every round.

The fight over the record is not new. The fight being on the record is.

None of that makes an account neutral. Somebody still decides what deserves an article and what is merely a detail.

But the deciding is now written down beside the decision. The chronicle has acquired a margin, and the margin is public.

Beria was removed with a razor blade, and there is no way left to see the cut.

Today it would be one grey line in a log, with a timestamp, and a name.
