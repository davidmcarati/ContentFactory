Somebody says the weather is depressing. Somebody else says the quarterly results are depressing. A third person, who has not left their flat in nine days and cannot remember why they used to like music, says they are a bit depressed.

All three used the same word. Only one of them was describing an illness.

Around two hundred and eighty million people are living with that illness. The World Health Organization ranks depressive disorders among the leading causes of disability worldwide. Not among mental illnesses. Among all causes, of any kind.

So it is worth being precise about what the word is supposed to mean.

The first thing to clear away is that depression is not sadness. Treating it as sadness with the volume turned up gets almost everything wrong.

Sadness has an object. You are sad about something, and when the something resolves, the sadness resolves with it. Sadness is also, oddly, useful. It slows you down. It makes you seek company. It signals to other people that they should be gentle with you.

Depression frequently has no object at all. And the symptom clinicians look for first is not sadness. It is anhedonia. The loss of the capacity for pleasure.

Anhedonia is a much stranger thing than sadness, and much harder to describe from the outside. Food tastes like it is being explained to you. Music becomes a sequence of correct notes. You can remember loving something without being able to feel it.

Some people with severe depression report that they cannot feel sad either. The condition took that as well.

None of this is a modern discovery. Physicians described it as melancholia for two thousand years. Albrecht Durer engraved it in fifteen fourteen: a winged figure sitting motionless, surrounded by every instrument of measurement and construction, using none of them.

It is one of the most accurate portraits of the condition anybody has made, and it predates the word depression by centuries.

So if it isn't sadness, what is it? Here is where almost everyone, quite reasonably, reaches for the same sentence. It's a chemical imbalance in the brain. Low serotonin.

That sentence has a history, and the history is not the one you would expect. It did not come out of a laboratory. It came, in large part, out of advertising.

Through the late eighties and the nineties, the drugs we now call SSRIs arrived, and with them a campaign to explain them to the public. The explanation had to fit inside a television commercial.

A chemical imbalance in the brain fits inside a television commercial. It is short, it is mechanical, and it puts the illness somewhere respectable. In the body, rather than in the character.

It was a simplification from the beginning, and researchers said so at the time. But it was an extremely good simplification, in the commercial sense, and it escaped.

In twenty twenty-two, a team led by Joanna Moncrieff published an umbrella review in Molecular Psychiatry. A study of studies, gathering the evidence for the serotonin hypothesis across six separate lines of research. They found no consistent evidence that depression is caused by low serotonin.

The press treated this as a demolition. Inside the field, the reaction was closer to a shrug, and the review itself drew serious methodological criticism. The simple version had been regarded as inadequate for years. What the review demolished was the commercial, not the science.

And here is the part that got mangled almost everywhere, so it is worth saying slowly.

Depression is not simply low serotonin does not mean antidepressants do not work. Those are two separate questions.

Aspirin works on headaches. Headaches are not caused by a lack of aspirin. A drug does not have to reverse a deficiency in order to help. If you are taking medication and it is helping you, a paper about serotonin is not a reason to stop.

So what is actually happening?

The honest answer is that depression is not one condition with one cause, which is precisely why the single-sentence explanation was so appealing and so wrong. But there are patterns, and they are better described as a system stuck in the wrong mode than as a missing ingredient.

The clearest of them is the stress system. The body runs a hormonal cascade through the hypothalamus, the pituitary and the adrenal glands. It produces cortisol, and then, when the threat has passed, it shuts itself off.

In a substantial share of depressed patients, the shutting off is impaired. The alarm keeps sounding, quietly, for months.

Cortisol is useful for an afternoon and corrosive over a year. Chronic elevation is associated with reduced volume in the hippocampus, the structure that handles memory and helps regulate the very stress response that is wearing it down. A loop that tightens on itself.

The second pattern is in the reward circuitry. Dopamine-carrying pathways running from the ventral tegmental area to the nucleus accumbens, and their conversation with the prefrontal cortex. This is the machinery that converts I want that into I am getting up.

Under sustained stress, that conversion degrades. Notice carefully what this means. It is not that the person wants to get up and lacks the willpower. It is that the step where wanting turns into doing has stopped working reliably. Anhedonia is that failure, experienced from the inside.

The third pattern is plasticity. The brain's capacity to remodel its own connections. Depression is associated with a measurable reduction in it, and several treatments that work appear to work by restoring it, over weeks rather than hours.

That timescale is itself a clue. It is the timescale of something being rebuilt, not of a level being topped up.

The fourth is inflammation. In a subset of patients, and it is a subset, inflammatory markers are elevated. Inducing inflammation experimentally in healthy volunteers produces the flat, unmotivated state of depression, along with changes in exactly the reward circuits just described.

Which suggests there are biologically distinct conditions currently wearing the same name.

Now the consequences. This is where the casual use of the word does the most damage.

Depression is not only felt. It measurably degrades the things people are held responsible for.

Concentration, working memory, decision speed and mental flexibility all decline. From the outside, this looks exactly like not trying. Someone misses deadlines. Forgets what was agreed. Takes four days to answer an email that would have taken four minutes.

The available explanations are ill and lazy, and the second one requires no imagination at all.

It does not stay in the head either. Depression is associated with higher cardiovascular risk and with worse outcomes for people already managing a physical illness, in ways that lifestyle alone does not account for.

And it carries a mortality risk of its own. Untreated depression is among the strongest predictors of suicide there is.

The World Health Organization puts the combined economic cost of depression and anxiety at up to a trillion dollars a year in lost productivity. That figure is usually quoted to make the case for funding treatment. It also quietly describes several hundred million people unable to do work they are otherwise capable of.

Which brings us to the part that is about us, rather than about neurons.

We have assembled a set of expectations around this illness that we would find absurd applied to any other.

We ask people whose reward circuitry has stopped converting motivation into action to be more motivated. We recommend exercise to someone who cannot start a shower. We tell them to reach out, and the reaching out costs more than they currently have.

And the strange part is that the advice is not even wrong. Exercise has real evidence behind it. Behavioural activation, deliberately doing things before you feel like doing them, is one of the better-supported treatments in the field.

The problem was never the content of the advice. It is handing somebody the operating instructions for a machine that is currently broken, and recording that as help delivered.

Then there is the word itself. A word that has to cover both a rainy Tuesday and a disabling illness will, in practice, get calibrated to the rainy Tuesday. That is simply how language works. The common meaning wins.

Which means that every time somebody says the real thing out loud, they are arguing with the definition before they even reach the end of the sentence.

Depression is not sadness, and it is not a missing chemical. It is a set of systems, stress regulation, motivation, plasticity, sometimes immunity, that have settled into a state they cannot leave unaided.

It is common. It is treatable. And it is far more mechanical, and far less voluntary, than the word makes it sound.

The word is the problem. The people using it about the weather are not.

But the next time somebody tells you they are depressed, it costs about four seconds to find out which of the two things they mean.
